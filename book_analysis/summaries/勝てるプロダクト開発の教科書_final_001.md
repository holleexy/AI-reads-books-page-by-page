## Book Overview: Taking Responsibility as a Product Owner

This book is written for people with the **resolve to take on responsibility**. Products fail not because of poor specifications or insufficient skill, but because it remains *ambiguous who ultimately takes responsibility*.

> **責任** (*responsibility*) means making the final decision amid uncertainty and never running away from explaining business outcomes. **引き受ける** (*taking it on*) means, when failure occurs, not blaming the market or other departments, but personally standing in the line of fire.

If that responsibility is not fulfilled and the product is defeated, the person must have the resolve to impose penalties on themselves—demotion or resignation—and not flee consequences involving the product’s survival, colleagues’ livelihoods, and market defeat.

### Audience and How to Use the Book

- **Primary audience:** product owners—PMs, business owners, and anyone accountable for product value and business success
- **Secondary audience:** specialists (engineers, designers, marketers, sales, PR, legal) who work with them
- Designed to be *used*, not merely read: opened, closed, and returned to in the middle of actual work
- Start from the chapter matching your current challenge; move back and forth; use it as a discussion starter
- Each chapter can be read independently, yet all are connected by one philosophy
- Chapters 1–11 each end with a *学びのリソース* (learning resources) section

**Author:** 蜂須賀大貴 (Hachisuka Daiki)—product development across startups, mega-ventures, and enterprise new businesses, as both field PM and decision-making product owner, experiencing growth and failure (including withdrawn products).

## Core Thesis and Unifying Question

Product failures were typically **not** caused by insufficient skills or frameworks. The real problems were simpler: unclear responsibility and stance.

Typical failure patterns:
- It was ambiguous who decided the market
- It was unclear who held customer understanding through to the end
- It was unknown who decided “we will not do this”
- It was ambiguous who would take responsibility for failure

> The book’s single unifying question across all chapters: **この判断の最終責任を、誰が引き受けるのか？** (*Who takes final responsibility for this judgment/decision?*)

This is a book about **stance** (*姿勢*), not job titles. PM, CPO, VPoP, CTO—titles are irrelevant. What matters is the attitude of the *プロダクト責任者* (person who takes product responsibility).

### Four Defining Stances of a Product Leader

1. Do not run away from customer value
2. Do not postpone decisions
3. Even when delegating, do not abandon responsibility
4. When failing, take responsibility instead of making excuses

**Intended residue after reading** is not mastery but lingering questions:
- What should I hold onto vs. what should I delegate?
- Where are the responsibility gaps in the current organization?
- Can I see customer value and business results at the same time?
- When making the next decision, can I *commit* (*腹を括る*)?

## The Product Owner’s Thinking OS (Preface)

A product manager needs a thinking **OS** that lets them correctly use knowledge across strategy, organization, technology, engineering, marketing, sales, PR, and legal. Individual skills and frameworks should be learned *only after* this OS is in place.

> Even an excellent application (strategy) will not function correctly if the OS is outdated or full of bugs.

### Three Foundational Abilities (*3つの基礎体力*)

These operate as a **linked process**, not independent skills:

1. **Logical thinking** (*ロジカルシンキング / 論理的思考力*) — impose order on chaotic situations
2. **Moving between concrete and abstract** (*具体と抽象を行き来する力 / 概念化能力*) — raise resolution; enable innovation
3. **Decision-making literacy** (*意思決定リテラシー / 判断の軸*) — guarantee decision quality across specialized domains

**Process:** Logical thinking captures structure of facts, interpretation, and action → moving between concrete and abstract raises resolution → decision-making literacy determines the next action.

### Logical Thinking in Practice

Not sounding smart or winning arguments. The power to organize chaotic situations in relation to purpose.

When trouble occurs, do **not** handle it emotionally (“push through with fighting spirit” / “scold the person in charge”). Immediately pose structural questions:

- Identify the **layer**: strategy mistake, tactics mistake, or operations error?
- Examine **timeline**: which process defect triggered the problem?
- Confirm **context**: what purpose was this function originally meant to achieve?
- List other variables (technical constraints, legal risk, user psychology)
- Language them as a tree, organize **MECE**, find the leverage point whose fix stops recurrence

Leaders who lack this bird’s-eye ability treat every event as both urgent and important—endless *whack-a-mole*. Analogous to engineering debugging: locate the root bug, not patch symptoms.

### Concrete ↔ Abstract Loop

Incoming product signals are always concrete (“User A cancelled,” “a request came for feature B”). Handling them only at that level yields *対症療法* (symptomatic treatment). You must abstract once, extract the essence, and transfer it.

**Reusable process:**
```
Concrete (own idea)
  → Abstract (extract essence)
  → Another concrete (success cases from other industries)
  → Re-concretize (apply to your own product)
```

**PIVOT example:** A “points program” looked like a mismatch for highly business-literate users. Abstract it: not “points for discounts” but *feedback/recognition* and *incentive design for continuation*. Raise abstraction further → airline miles, Amex-style status. Redefining as “miles/status” made it resonate.

This loop is the dividing line between a mediocre product owner and an excellent one.

### Decision-Making Literacy

A product owner is the final decision-maker, yet cannot possess expert-level knowledge across every domain. What is required is not specialized skills themselves, but a **judgment axis** that lets you engage experts as equals and evaluate risk vs. return.

Four types of questions a non-expert should pose to specialists:
1. **What to ask** — specialized knowledge, cases, unique judgment criteria
2. **What to decide** — what is optimal for *this* company, how far to go considering the whole
3. **Where the risks are** — existing violations, licenses that conflict with schedule
4. **What to delegate vs. hold** — materials needed; respective roles in decision-making

**Financial literacy is unavoidable:** P/L, B/S, C/F structures; gross profit; CAPEX vs. OPEX. These are basic literacy for knowing when to accelerate or brake.

The product owner cannot abdicate by fully outsourcing legal or engineering. Even without knowing statutes, they must know which laws are fundamental to the business model; even without writing code, they must understand how technical debt affects the business.

> Expert advice is usually correct *within its domain*. The product owner judges with two lenses: **domain correctness × business correctness**. Final responsibility always stays with you.

## Delegating vs. Dumping

- **Delegating** (*任せる*): keep outcome responsibility yourself while giving ownership to experts
- **Dumping** (*丸投げ*): hand over both outcome responsibility *and* thinking, isolating the experts

Dumping makes the locus of responsibility unclear and almost certainly causes the business to fail.

### Six Elements to Align Before Handing Off Work

1. **背景** — background/premise: why this initiative is needed *now*
2. **目的** — purpose: whose problem will ultimately be solved
3. **判断基準** — decision axis: what to prioritize vs. discard
4. **期待値** — expected role and output quality
5. **期限** — deadline plus *why* that date was set
6. **リスクと不確実性** — visible concerns plus unverified black boxes

Without these: *ブラックボックス化* (black-boxing), quality degradation, *属人化* (person-dependency), inability to explain; the ball drops at role boundaries.

### Five Criteria the Product Owner Must Hold (Diagram 0-2)

1. **目的 (Why)** — state the why forcefully, even if work looks irrational
2. **制約条件** — cost, schedule, legal, technical debt; constraints sit behind passion
3. **成功指標** — KPI/KGI; without metrics it is impossible to know which axis to improve
4. **代替案 (Options)** — always consider alternatives together with risks
5. **リスク / Worst Case** — think through scenarios in advance so you can respond with composure

**Questioning skill** when working with experts:
- “Why that approach?” — logic for judging it optimal for *your* company
- “What other options were there?” — alternatives considered and rejected
- What risks a choice creates (future technical debt, business trade-offs)
- Worst-case: recovery possibility; how fatal the damage
- How it could be even better if current constraints were removed

As an organization grows, the product owner must shift from personally executing to raising the quality of decision-making. The more people one delegates to, the heavier the remaining decisions become. **The ability to delegate is career-defining.**

## Chapter 1: Market Selection (*市場選び*)

> **Core principle:** Choosing a winnable market determines everything. Even superior strategy, tactics, or a strong team will fail if the market itself is not winnable.

**BlackBerry case:** Dominated “efficient email via physical keyboard.” The iPhone created a new battlefield—“intuitive touch plus app extensibility”—after which the contest was decided regardless of BlackBerry’s team quality. A complete change in market wind cannot be resisted.

### Three Conditions for a Winnable Market

1. **Growing market** — ride an upward escalator; new or redefined market
2. **Absence of an overwhelming No.1** — multiple players, no dominant leader yet
3. **Exclusive/monopolistic position (MOAT)** — unique standing via proprietary technology or assets; potential for platformization

Selecting the opposite starts one at a strategic disadvantage: shrinking/stagnant markets; mature markets with high entry barriers; markets already monopolized.

A stagnant market *can* still be chosen deliberately if the plan is to change industry customs via DX or AI—but only because the situation has been correctly read.

Market selection = choosing the **difficulty level of the game**. First-time leaders should start in lower-difficulty markets and expand horizontally. Serial entrepreneurs increasingly jump into highest-difficulty markets. The first challenge is knowing one’s own capabilities (*身の程*).

### Information Gathering as War Preparation (*戦支度*)

Markets are living systems. Intermittent one-shot research cannot capture the whole picture.

- Always overview **widely and deeply**: Nikkei, stock prices, overseas cases (time-machine management)
- After narrowing: industry-specific problems news does not cover
- Attend industry events; use AI research tools; hear operators’ unfiltered language
- Mental model: understand the market like a **river’s flow**—upstream and downstream ecosystems, weather and wind

Do not spare time selecting a winnable market. Keep researching until you are personally convinced.

### Passion × Market Understanding (2×2 Framework)

Choosing a battlefield is finding the intersection of **one’s own passion** and **market potential**.

|  | Current market | Future market prediction |
|--|--|--|
| **Past experience / strengths** | *専門性の発揮* — Demonstrating Expertise (highest certainty) | *先行優位の構築* — Building First-Mover Advantage |
| **Future vision / aspiration** | *社会的意義の追求* — Pursuing Social Significance | *未踏領域の開拓* — Pioneering Uncharted Territory |

What matters is not which domain is “correct,” but being conscious of which you are trying to win in.

**原体験** (*original/formative experience*) supplies lasting motivation. It need not be positive. No one truly lacks one; it is merely unverbalized. **Vision Quest**: retreat-style session with only paper and pen to surface it.

Neither original experience nor corporate vision is inherently more correct. Both sustain the self. Without a genuine reason to persist, the effort will not last.

### Revising Market–Solution Fit When the Battlefield Is Already Set

When a PM joins mid-stream, the market is often already decided. The product owner must have the **courage to verify even management’s initial settings** without treating them as sacred.

Two approaches:
- **Market-in:** remake the solution to fit the market’s problem (keep market, pivot product)
- **Product-out:** search for the market in which one’s own solution/strengths will shine most brightly

The product leader’s work is to move back and forth between problem and solution, hunting for the sweet spot where the business can actually win.

### Roles with Executives and Business Owners

Clarify three parties: executives (*経営陣*), business leaders (*事業責任者*), and product-management function.

- **Launch phase:** entrepreneur/business leader who owns the product should personally take the PM role. PM’s most important job: install the founder’s vision into themselves—become the founder’s right hand, driver, and translator.
- **Growth phase:** two patterns—executive personally takes charge as new-business leader (e.g., CyberAgent’s Fujita directing ABEMA), or management appoints a dedicated business-unit leader.

**CEO-type product leader:** decision-making principal spanning market, organization, finance, legal, development.
**COO-type product leader:** execution owner who translates vision into the field.

### Initiative vs. Delegation

Initiative (*イニシアティブ*) is not gathering opinions and making the final decision. It is first showing one’s own will and indicating direction.

**Always hold:** vision, market, and customer problems—even at 50 or 100 people. If you are no longer the most knowledgeable person on these, have the resolve to hand the role to someone else.

**Delegate:** technical selection/architecture (if CTO/tech lead exists); concrete UI/UX (hold direction, leave craft); marketing/sales tactics (hold strategy and KPI design).

In high-uncertainty domains: first try doing it yourself to build intuition (*勘所*). Only after you can delegate with reproducibility should you transfer.

**Product Owner Involvement Level Map** (strategy / tactics / measures × startup / growth / expansion):
- Startup: hold strategy and tactics yourself; jointly hold measures
- Growth: jointly hold strategy and tactics; delegate measures
- Expansion: jointly hold strategy; delegate tactics and measures

### Six Market-Selection Failure Patterns

1. **Passion alone** — over-relying on *原体験*; markets that generate empathy but lack economic growth potential burn out (often after Series A–B)
2. **Overcrowded booming market** — chasing first-mover advantage; weak differentiation → price-and-feature competition; Big Tech later injects polished products
3. **Premature market** — doing a business ten years too early; high enlightenment costs. Customers imagine only one step ahead; nurture a *half-step-ahead* preceding market
4. **No real customers** — “desk chimera” (*机上のキメラ*): stitching company-convenient features into a fictional persona
5. **Unbalanced thinking** — gut-feel with no research *or* over-analysis that delays GO timing. Inactive PMs and unthinking PMs are equally dangerous
6. **Overconfidence** — initial success becomes a curse; strategy not updated; customer experience updates (e.g., AI) while the product cannot change

**Defenses:** do not run on passion alone; examine economic viability; in a red ocean, design MOAT; enter from half a step ahead; validate with real customers; cycle think-then-move; continuously update strategy.

## Chapter 2: Vision and Business Development

After a market is chosen, the first step of strategy is answering **Why me / Why now**.

### Why Me — Necessity That *You* Must Do It

Three overlapping connections:
1. **Original experience** — past pain, challenges, strong emotion
2. **Unique assets** — network, technology, experience, domain knowledge (*非代替性* / non-substitutability)
3. **Ideology** — the social change you want to create

Storytelling sequence: past (original experience) → present (strengths/assets) → future.

### Why Now — Necessity of *This Timing*

Three components:
1. Changes in technology, institutions, and infrastructure
2. Changes in society and culture
3. Changes in competitive environment and industrial structure

Being too early or too late are both too late. Get on the board at the rising moment.

> Why me and Why now are each insufficient alone. Combining both produces the core of business strategy: **Why this business**.

When you can answer both in your own words, strategy design has already begun. Purpose becomes vision—not a top-down slogan, but something that emerges by refining questions.

### Collecting and Reconstituting Business Ideas

Ideas are crystallizations of thinking from experience and knowledge—not flashes of inspiration.

**Three daily habits (Diagram 2-4):**
1. Observe one product/business every day—touch it, ask why the experience succeeds
2. Decompose the business model and stock it (price design, acquisition funnel, monetization)
3. Language which *layer* was the key: customer-problem solving, technology use, or revenue-structure innovation

**Three perspectives for learning from other companies:**
- *手触りで観る* — hands-on experience as a user
- *構造で観る* — numbers, time axes, organizational structure
- *思想で観る* — relive founders’ vision, decisions, failure stories

**Good vs. bad imitation:**
- Bad: superficial copy of design/features without philosophy (e.g., copying Timee’s matching accuracy without its on-site DX sales organization)
- Good: redesign mechanisms, philosophy, and context; localize to the company; stay consistent with Why me
