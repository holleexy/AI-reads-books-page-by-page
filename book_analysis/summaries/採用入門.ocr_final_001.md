## Core Philosophy of HR and Recruitment

The core definition of *人事* (HR) is:

> **「人を生かして事をなす」** — accomplishing work by fully utilizing people (bringing their capabilities to life).

Two failures of this definition:
- **「人を犠牲にしてでも、事をなす」** is not HR; it is *搾取* (exploitation).
- **「人がイキイキとしていても、事がなされない」** is not HR; it is *ぬるま湯* (a lukewarm, complacent environment).

True HR is the **simultaneous realization of both 人 and 事**: each person’s abilities are fully demonstrated *and* the organization’s purpose is achieved. Here *事* refers to *成果* (results).

Recruitment (*採用*) is not merely gathering people or increasing headcount. It is an endeavor in which an organization and people encounter each other and **together shape the future**. The orientation should shift from *「人を選ぶ」* (selecting people) to *「関係性を築く」* (building relationships).

Even in large, long-established companies, everyone except the founder entered through recruitment. Hiring is therefore a key that creates the company’s future and that shapes organizational culture and growth speed.

## Understanding Recruitment: Four Sequential Elements

To understand recruitment correctly, examine four elements **in sequence**:

1. **Results (*成果*)** — Per P.F. Drucker, the value an organization provides to customers and society. What matters is not internal effort but value visible from outside. *“Results exist outside the organization”* (*The Practice of Management*).
2. **Business (*事業*)** — The continuous activity of providing value: creating products/services and receiving revenue. The overall mechanisms that enable a company to grow while delivering offerings.
3. **Organization (*組織*)** — Required to keep producing results through business. The characters mean “weaving together”: people linking toward a common purpose, dividing roles, and collaborating. HR functions are indispensable for the organization to function smoothly. Recruitment is the **first and especially important core HR function**.
4. **Recruitment (*採用*)** — Bringing in the talent needed so the organization can achieve results through its business. If the hired person fails to adapt, contribute to the business, or produce results, the recruitment has **failed**.

Done well, recruitment injects expertise, new ideas, and previously missing capabilities, accelerating growth. Future executives must also be hired; they do not otherwise exist inside the organization.

## Four Impacts of Recruitment

Recruitment has impacts at three levels (organization, individual, society):

- **Organizational growth**: Two types — *増員* (staff increase for expansion/new projects) and *補充* (replacement of vacancies, often needing *即戦力* / immediate contributors).
- **Organizational activation**: Settled order leads to inactivation. New talent introduces *chaos (disorder)* that disrupts implicit rules and reactivates the organization (related to chaos theory / Osawa Takeshi’s *Psychological Management*).
- **Individual career formation**: Provides opportunities to demonstrate abilities and grow. In Japan’s shrinking labor market (seller’s market), workers must perceive the opportunity as advantageous for their own careers (linked to recruitment CX).
- **Social employment creation**: Provides stable employment indispensable to society. Restraint during Japan’s *就職氷河期* (employment ice age) greatly shook the foundation of Japanese society.

> Recruitment grows and activates the organization, helps working individuals’ career formation, and creates employment in society.

## Overall Structure of Recruitment

Recruitment is structured around three components:

1. **Recruitment Team** — HR personnel, field managers, field members, executives, and external partners collaborating.
2. **Recruitment Process** (the short-term “Arrow Feather Model” / *矢羽モデル*) — *採用要件* (requirements) → *募集/sourcing* → *選考* (selection) → *適応* (adaptation/onboarding).
3. **Recruitment CX (Candidate Experience)** — Everything the candidate experienced at all touchpoints from application through joining. Speedy communication, careful responses, and moments of feeling company culture influence offer-acceptance and post-joining adaptation.

Supporting layers:
- *採用マーケティング* (recruitment marketing) — nurtures the candidate–company relationship.
- *採用戦略* (recruitment strategy) — aligns management/business strategy with candidates’ needs: when, what kind of people, how many, and how.

Recruitment operates across **short-term and long-term** horizons. Study method: first grasp the overall structure broadly, then learn each constituent element.

## History of Japanese Recruitment

Periodization (*図表 004*):

| Period | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| **Meiji** | Western-style, academic-credential elite hiring; government offices and zaibatsu; limited elite stratum |
| **Taisho / early Showa** | Mass recruitment of factory workers and clerical staff; foundation of lifetime employment |
| **Post-WWII reconstruction** | Explicit long-term employee development; Japanese-style employment crystallizes around new-graduate hiring, mass simultaneous recruitment, seniority, in-company education |
| **High-growth era (1950s–1970s)** | Joining a large firm = “stability and success”; *就職協定* and school recommendations; *新卒一括採用* established |
| **1970s–1980s** | Lifetime employment and internal training taken for granted; viewed as distinctive Japanese strength |
| **1990s (bubble collapse)** | Limits of lifetime employment/seniority exposed; growth of experienced-hire, dispatch, contract; abolition of job agreements; liberalization of hiring calendars |
| **21st century** | IT and globalization diversify hiring; year-round recruitment and internships expand; foreign-affiliated and venture firms adopt *ジョブ型* and meritocracy; work-style reform; remote hiring and online interviews |

Japanese hiring has shifted from *大量一括* (mass simultaneous) toward *個別最適* (individual optimization) to respond to diversified talent needs. Practices continue to evolve daily.

Classic references: Abegglen’s *The Japanese Factory* (1958); Koike Kazuo’s *仕事の経済学* (1991); Saguchi Kazuo’s *雇用システム論* (2018); Kondo Ayako on the employment ice-age generation (2024).

## Japan vs. United States Hiring Models

| Dimension | Japan | United States |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | *新卒一括採用* (mass simultaneous new-graduate hiring timed to graduation) | *通年採用* (year-round hiring for specific open positions) |
| **Criteria** | Organizational fit and *potential* more than current skills; student activities and personality | Immediate *skills* and *results*; job-specific tests (coding, case interviews) |
| **Employment** | *長期雇用* + *ジョブローテーション* → generalists; weeks-to-months of collective new-graduate training | *ジョブ型雇用*; individuals drive their own careers; tailored, self-directed training |
| **Advantages** | Organizational capability, belonging, loyalty; room to grow together | Right person in the right role; deepened expertise; market fluidity |
| **Disadvantages** | Slow adaptation to change; restricted hiring windows | Weaker employment stability; intense competition; higher turnover |

> Differences in hiring practices by country originate from culture and employment systems.

Japan’s model is *membership-style, potential-oriented*; America’s is *job-oriented, skills-and-results*. Trade-off: internal growth/cohesion vs. talent matching/specialization plus market fluidity.

## New-Graduate vs. Experienced-Hire Recruitment

### New-graduate hiring (*新卒採用*)

Unique to Japan, understood in relation to *終身雇用* and *年功序列*. Companies hire large numbers of students at a fixed time, selecting primarily on **potential** toward future growth. Typical process: apply via job sites → document screening + aptitude tests → multiple interviews (final often with executives) → *内定*. Characteristically longer contact period than mid-career.

Long-term development is assumed: concentrated training, job rotation to develop generalists, same-year *同期* cohort that shares culture and unity. Emerging shifts: year-round recruitment, scouting, internships (sometimes locking candidates early), *ジョブ型採用*, online interviews.

### Experienced hiring (*経験者採用*)

Targets people with prior work experience; evaluates **practical experience and specialized skills**. Premise difference: new graduates are hired to *育てる* (nurture); experienced hires are hired to *任せる* (entrust). Immediate performance after joining is the most important factor.

Demand is fluid and often sudden (vacancy replacement, new-business launches). Field/business units often lead; HR supports. Assignment is frequently already decided. Contact is limited; selection is individualized (1-on-1, meals). Must also *win against competing employers*. Growing emphasis on adaptability, diversified values, offensive methods (scouting, referrals), and post-hire onboarding.

## Common Hiring Failures

Four typical Japanese-company failure patterns, mapped to Nonaka et al.’s *失敗の本質* (Japanese military in the Pacific War):

1. **Ambiguous requirements** — “we want excellent people”; skill mismatch, early turnover. Analog: successive short-term operations.
2. **Overly personalized selection** — interviewers overweight intuition and likes/dislikes. Analog: personalized leadership / commander’s intuition.
3. **Gap between recruitment messaging and workplace reality** — over-beautified PR; “this is different from what I was told.” Analog: HQ-style information manipulation + optimism.
4. **Lack of strategy** — filling immediate vacancies, never building a future leadership layer. Analog: no comprehensive strategy / local-battle orientation.

> Obsession with temporary tactical success, failure to create fundamental strategy, and reliance on personal judgment plus excessive “appearance” rebound as large medium-to-long-term failure.

Core principle from Tsutomu Okamoto: **never stop hiring, even when business performance is poor.** A two-year freeze produces a hole in the age/experience pyramid, leaving no successors for middle-management roles around age 40. Personnel composition records history like tree rings. Stopping hiring is also reputationally damaging (“that company has stopped hiring” mutates into “their performance is in trouble”). Better to solicit voluntary retirements and refresh the workforce than simply freeze new hiring.

## Recruitment as an HR Function: The Holon Structure

HR consists of overlapping functions (labor affairs, talent management, organization development, MBO) arranged in a **holon structure** (Ken Wilber’s Integral Theory): nested, ring-like hierarchy in which lower layers support upper layers while upper layers encompass the lower ones (“transcend and include”). Occupying an upper layer does not make a person “superior.” If a lower layer fails, layers above cannot function.

- If labor affairs fail, talent-management investments are wasted (livelihood, health, internal order disrupted).
- If talent management fails, organization development produces a *ぬるま湯* organization that only talks and declines.

Recruitment’s role within the holon is to bring in **“the people who accomplish things.”** As the lowest layer it is the starting point of everything — the most fundamental root function. If recruitment does not work, talent simply does not exist. Even if highly talented people are hired, they will not take root unless other layers also work.

> Every personnel function exists to bring out people’s potential in order to accomplish work.

Role-specific messages:
- **Executives**: Recruitment is the most important theme directly connected to executing management strategy; the entrance that shapes the organization’s future.
- **Field managers**: Recruitment is not only HR’s job; they must own who to hire, how to develop, and how to retain.
- **Recruitment officers**: Stand on the front line connecting company and candidates; design the full experience including branding, selection, and retention.
- **All members**: Everyone has been welcomed and will eventually stand on the welcoming side. Hiring is not someone else’s business.

## The Recruitment Team

Hiring should be conducted as a **team**, not by individuals.

### Three merits of team-based hiring
- **A. Diverse perspectives prevent mismatches** — executives (strategy), field managers (operational reality), recruiters (market + CX), external partners (talent pool).
- **B. Collaboration balances speed and quality** — parallel processing of intake, scheduling, evaluation, offer negotiation; standardized criteria.
- **C. Team learning converts results into organizational assets** — sharing post-interview questionnaires, accumulating good questions.

### Five conditions for a learning hiring team
1. Common purpose and goals
2. Clear role division (RACI-style)
3. Open information (typically via ATS)
4. Habit of retrospectives
5. Candidate-centered approach (*採用CX*)

### Five types of recruitment-team structure
1. **Decentralized / field-led** — founding-stage, small orgs; fast but inconsistent CX.
2. **Dedicated / centralized** — in-house recruiting team; consistent brand; risk of field mismatch.
3. **Hybrid / matrix** — dedicated team owns planning/pool; field owns interviews. Rapid-growth companies. Role ambiguity risk.
4. **Project / time-limited** — mixed team for finite high-volume need; know-how must be captured.
5. **Outsource / RPO** — external specialists run operations; internal team focuses on planning/final decisions. Over-reliance leaves no in-house know-how.

Migration by headcount: ≤50 → decentralized; >100 → dedicated; >300 → hybrid. Structure must be regularly reviewed.

### Four roles (*図表 013*)
- **経営者 (executives)**: show direction — approve talent requirements linked to strategy, set budget/ROI, personally communicate employer brand.
- **現場マネージャー (field managers)**: guarantee workplace fit — define position requirements, evaluate skills/stance, create onboarding plans.
- **外部パートナー (external partners)**: raise efficiency — candidate pools, market information (segments, competitor movements, compensation rates).
- **採用担当者 (recruiter)**: hub connecting management, field, and external parties — strategy/process design, progress monitoring, CX quality.

Role division is made durable through a **write–share–update** cycle. Also cultivate awareness of the shared *ba* (place of dialogue).

### Executives’ four roles
1. Show direction (reverse-calculate strategy into talent needs)
2. Show your face (SNS, events, videos — lifts application and offer-acceptance)
3. Decide quickly (zero-wait offer/budget flow; treat hiring as investment)
4. Talk about culture (embody uniqueness; evaluation systems that back the claim)

> When top leadership commits to recruitment, the hiring activity is already half successful.

### Field managers’ five roles
1. Requirement verbalization (concrete actions/results, not abstract traits)
2. Assessing skills and stance (behavioral examples + culture-fit questions; evaluation sheets)
3. Staging CX (honest talk about challenges; Slack invites)
4. Adaptation/onboarding support (30/90-day goals, mentor, weekly follow-ups)
5. Result verification (performance, retention, engagement → update requirements)

### External partners’ four types
- **Job media**: mass exposure + market sensor (view/click histories of latent candidates)
- **Talent-introduction agencies**: quality guarantee + market-rate insight; success fee typically 30–35% of annual salary
- **Scout / direct recruiting**: same-day pinpoint outreach; iterate on reply data
- **RPO**: full process outsourcing with SLA/KPI; internal team concentrates on strategy and CX

> Building a relationship of trust with external partners is the key to successful recruitment.

### Recruiter as multi-functional player (five roles)
1. Recruitment Strategy Planner
2. Recruitment Process Designer
3. Information Coordinator
4. CX Designer
5. Organizational Learning Facilitator

Required **results**: fill rate (quantity) + post-hire performance/retention (quality). Example: “Hire 5 engineers during Q2 and maintain 90% retention after six months.”

Five skill domains: labor law/contracts; recruitment process; interview/evaluation; data literacy; tool operation (ATS, scout media, AI matching). Stance suited to **emotional labor**: sincerity, curiosity, persistence, composure.

Career paths: HR manager/HRBP; Talent Acquisition specialist; freelance/consultant/RPO; emerging
