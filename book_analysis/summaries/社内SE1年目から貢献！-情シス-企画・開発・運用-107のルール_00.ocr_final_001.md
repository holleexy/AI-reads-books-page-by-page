## Context and Challenges for First-Year In-House SEs
In-house SEs (*社内SE*) face two core struggles: superiors and seniors who teach nothing, and uncertainty about what to study. These arise from **structural conditions**, not individual failings.

- Rising IT demand creates chronic resource shortages as numerous projects land on in-house teams.
- Business companies prioritize projects over internal education, so bosses allocate time to delivery rather than teaching.
- In Japan, ~70% of IT talent belongs to vendor firms (*SIers*); only ~30% work in user/business companies (the reverse of overseas ratios). Most Japanese educational materials target *SIers* and poorly match in-house work.
- Remote work has reduced informal learning (watching seniors or casual conversation).
- First-year in-house SEs are also first-year working adults with a large volume of material to absorb amid information overflow.

Efficient learning requires first identifying knowledge and skills especially demanded of a first-year in-house SE. Expectations equal necessary knowledge/skills and arise from three layers: the current state of Japanese business, IT expectations of operating (non-IT) companies, and global IT trends.

> Internal SEs must themselves change in response to environmental shifts. Those who succeeded as traditional in-house SEs often find past success obstructs necessary change. ## Chapter 1: What an In-House SE Is and How the Role Will Evolve
We are in the **4th Industrial Revolution**. The 20th-century 3rd Revolution used PCs and the Internet to automate manufacturing work (e.g., ERP packages); in-house SEs promoted company IT adoption. The 21st century connects things via IoT, big data, and AI for optimization. IT now permeates every domain; in-house SEs support both internal systems *and* external digital-service provision. Some executives have shifted to *IT-centric management* (*IT経営*), treating IT as the axis of strategy (e.g., Amazon, Rakuten).

The term originated from Germany’s *Industrie 4.0* (Hannover Messe 2011). National programs followed (UK Catapult Centres, US Advanced Manufacturing Partnership, China Made in China 2025, Japan Society 5.0). COVID-19 further accelerated digitalization from “necessary” to “inevitable,” including among SMEs.

Japan’s declining birthrate and aging population (*少子高齢化*) uniquely accelerate IT need. Population peaked at 127.84 million in December 2004 and is projected to fall to ~47.71 million by 2100 (aging rate 40%). Systems assuming population growth (welfare, pensions, business models) must transform; IT is the critical supporting role. Shrinking domestic resources plus expanding global markets make IT indispensable for borderless operations.

**Company IT-promotion attitude** can be read from investment (guideline ~1% of sales, though industry and lifecycle vary) and organization charts:
- Information Systems as a subsection of General Affairs (no CIO) → low awareness/appetite.
- Dedicated IT department headed by a CIO, especially with a DX-promotion organization → clear intent for *offensive IT* (*攻めのIT*).

Contrast *traditional IT investment* (cost reduction, safety-first, backend, waterfall) vs. *offensive IT* (sales/added value, speed-first, frontend, Agile/DevOps). First-years must understand their IT department’s current state, direction, and “how hard the accelerator is pressed.” Gaps may justify external learning or future job change. The book’s basic rules remain useful regardless of organizational IT aggressiveness.

In-house SEs belong to their *business company’s industry*, not the IT industry. They interact mainly with *SIer*-type firms. The IT industry comprises five sectors (Internet/Web, communications, package software, hardware, information-processing services). Key job titles include System Engineer, IT Consultant, Network Engineer, Security Engineer, Programmer, Tester, and IT Project Manager.

Distinguish *SIer SE* (vendor, mainly upstream for clients), *社内SE* (business company, wide range from development through operations), and *SES* (typically client-stationed quasi-mandate support). Typical chain: in-house SE (requester) → large *SIer* (prime) → SES *SIer* (subcontractor). New development is often commissioned to *SIers*; enhancements consult existing operations vendors.

Contribution is **value to the business**, not the highest-quality system. Understand the profit-and-reinvestment cycle: mission/vision → strategy → investment → products/services → sales → profit (reinvested, including IT). IT solutions are a *means*, not the end. Infer project purpose from proposing department (sales increase, cost reduction/compliance, or maintenance/risk). Do not treat system-building as the goal.

Two employment models: *job-type* (roles defined first, specialists, high mobility) vs. *membership-type* (work assigned to people, bulk new-graduate hiring, rotation). The book addresses first-years under membership-type employment. Broad experience later enables moves to job-type firms or *SIers*.

Internal information is practical and culture-adapted but ages quickly. External gathering (e.g., the author’s blog) is essential. Balance both.

## Chapter 2: Skills Required of an In-House SE
Chapter 2 answers: What is needed to produce results? Which skills should a first-year acquire? How to study IT skills efficiently?

An adapted **Katz model** categorizes skills as *conceptual* (*コンセプチュアルスキル*), *management*, *human* (*ヒューマンスキル*), and *technical* (*テクニカルスキル*). In-house work sits at the intersection of business-department knowledge and IT.

**Structure for raising results**: *成果* (what to achieve) + *戦略* (what to prioritize) + *戦術* (how to execute), supported by knowledge, skills, awareness, and attitude. Simply raising skills does not produce results if actions do not connect to outcomes.

- Strategy has two levels: company/department strategy (cascaded from vision) and individual goals.
- Motivation: *intrinsic* (dreams/aspirations, sustainable) vs. *extrinsic* (rewards/punishments, temporary). Link high-perspective intrinsic motives to assigned work (parable of the three bricklayers: the one building a historic cathedral works with highest awareness).
- First-years often receive abstract requests; convert them into personal goals aligned with aspirations. Attach motive to whatever work arrives rather than waiting for “dream” tasks.
- Immediately usable high-perspective methods: multi-faceted/bird’s-eye viewpoint and abstract thinking.

**Technical skills** (immediately applicable): IT-industry knowledge, IT technical skills (development, infrastructure, operations, management), industry knowledge, business knowledge, and business-model understanding. For manufacturing, grasp Engineering Chain (PLM/PDM) and Supply Chain (MES, CRM, SCM, ERP).

**Conceptual skills** (grasp essence and overcome issues): logical thinking, critical thinking, lateral thinking, multi-faceted viewpoint, *俯瞰力* (overview ability). Also intellectual curiosity, inquiry, receptivity, flexibility, challenge spirit, insight, and foresight. Especially effective for daily problem-solving (business challenges, failures, user dissatisfaction). Imitate strong thinkers as a lightweight training method.

**Human skills** (build/maintain relationships): communication, hearing, negotiation, motivation, presentation, leadership, coaching. For first-years, **customer perspective** (*顧客視点*) and **respect toward the other party** (*相手への敬意*) are most effective. Prioritize preventing inconvenience to the company’s customers (“stop the bleeding”) over debating defect vs. specification. Treat everyone equally regardless of age/title (Konosuke Matsushita: everyone is a diamond in the rough). Job titles are almost useless outside that company.

Management skills combine the above and target people, things, money, and information; first-years typically manage limited routine work or a project.

**Learning efficiency**: Finite time requires prioritizing immediately usable skills. Stop something to create time; act while thinking (accept “three-day monk” starts); turn learning into habits (behavioral ~1 month, physical ~3 months, thinking ~6 months). Reflection + systematization doubles experience value from the same event. Study along SE-oriented IT qualifications (national, vendor, private) as an efficient path with abundant materials; certifications are a *byproduct*, not the goal. No qualifications are mandatory. Vendor certs for the company’s actual cloud/SaaS (AWS/Azure cover ~60% share) are especially efficient. National exams: Fundamental Information Technology Engineer and Project Manager.

> Simply raising skills does not lead to results. Align personal goals with corporate strategy so company work also moves you toward the person you want to become.

## Chapter 3: Operations/Maintenance and Project Management
In-house SE work splits into team-specific duties and common duties (steady operations and project-common work). First-years are often assigned operations/maintenance and project management as a *gateway* (*登竜門*) because they can be performed with relatively broad/shallow knowledge.

**Operations/maintenance** is essential for daily IT operation and a treasure trove of opportunities: knowledge expansion (service-desk inquiries across domains), network expansion (contact with users and experts), and problem-solving skill (apply conceptual skills). Roles include *システム運用*, *システム保守*, *業務運用保守*, *サービスデスク*, and *ヘルプデスク*. Understand the company’s actual functional placement against a general model.

End-to-end handling: user → business ops/maintenance (triage) → IT help desk (incident registration) → ops/maintenance (possibly with *SIer*) → resolution back to user. Non-IT requests go via service desk.

Practical advice: Hate the sin, not the sinner (focus on root cause, not irritated attitudes). Teamwork—do not handle every incident alone. Execute *should-do* tasks, not merely *can-do* tasks. Use a four-quadrant framework (importance vs. urgency). Warren Buffett: “Tasks other than the ones you should do interfere with the ones you should do.”

**Project management** is limited-scope resource management (people, things, money) and a gateway to larger organizational steering. Purpose is to take necessary actions to lead the project to success; management is a *means*, not the goal. Visualize information so judgments can be formed.

Key items: progress, resource, budget, issue, risk, milestone, communication, deliverable, software/hardware management.

- **Progress management**: Compare plan vs. actual to recognize issues and prompt action. Understand overall plan/milestones, identify own-area work (do not rely only on *SIer* WBS), agree dates, then manage. Clarify owner and due date. Use project-wide tools/templates. Align on deliverable meaning. Reconcile cross-team dependencies.
- **WBS**: Hierarchical decomposition (large/medium/small classification) with assignees, progress, dates. Confirm existing templates/tools first. Apply MECE. Internal SE must enumerate full internal tasks, not only *SIer* scope. Create realistic plans; escalate if the WBS already shows the deadline cannot be met. Reuse past examples.
- **Resource management**: Understand *工数*, *人日* (8 hours), *人月* (20 days), FTE. Estimate effort by phase/month, then assign people. Purpose is optimal allocation to advance the project.
- **Budget management**: “Don’t let the gasoline run dry.” Track plan vs. actual; understand investment vs. expenses, approval/*稟議*. Never let an *SIer* start without contract and internal approval. Distinguish *準委任* (pay for work performed) vs. *請負* (pay for deliverable). Protect the budget spreadsheet from vendor access. Escalate shortfalls immediately.
- **Issue management**: Resolve by deadlines (“don’t let the wound widen”). Precursor detection. Track expected completion, owner, response, impact, status. Specific descriptions. High-impact first. Visualization is not the goal—resolution is.
- **Communication management**: Mechanisms so communication proceeds smoothly. Distinguish project (organization-chart-based) vs. organizational (company-chart, 1-on-1s) communication. Both required. Situational choice of oral (speed, reading reaction) vs. written (accuracy, formal, volume).
- **Deliverable management**: Broad definition includes meeting materials, WBS, issue tables, etc. Define storage rules before start; align with IT-department practices. Standardize final-deliverable location; allow team discretion for working documents. Folder-by-phase, naming conventions, access rights, common templates. Document and communicate rules (especially important with turnover).
- **Software/hardware management**: Balanced control of asset-class or confidentiality-risk items (PCs, IDs, network gear, restricted licenses). Ledger columns: category, item, number, dates, user, manager, confirmation, expiration, location, return deadline. Report *SIer* personnel changes.

> Operations and maintenance is a chance to acquire broad business *and* system knowledge and to expand personal networks. Project management’s purpose is using management to prompt action and succeed.

## Chapter 4: Overall Picture of System Construction
System construction sits under business strategy → IT strategy → project policy. Four axes of approach choices:

1. **Business improvement/reform**: localized *業務改善*, organization-wide *BPR*, *デジタル化* (digitize existing operations), *DX* (digitally enabled business-model change).
2. **System construction**: enhance (short, partial improvement; common first-year assignment), scratch (longer, flexible but black-box risk), package introduction, cloud/SaaS utilization, RPA.
3. **SIer utilization**: in-house, IT support only, business+IT support, complete outsourcing (*丸投げ*—avoid).
4. **Development methodology**: waterfall (easy management/quality/planning), agile (small units, speedy release, fewer deliverables; suited to web, less to physical ops), hybrid (e.g., waterfall early phases + agile construction).

Projects comprise four phases: proposal (*起案*), launch (*立ち上げ*), construction (*構築*), release/operation. First-years typically join one limited area of one phase and must understand preceding/following connections. Collaboration is essential; an in-house SE should never execute business design + construction + testing + release alone.

Five external-resource patterns exist. Patterns ④ and ⑤ (*丸投げ*) produce unused systems or wasted money/time and must be escalated. Package/cloud projects should fit business to the package (Fit to Standard), use small-start, and readily apply agile.

> Differences in business-improvement methods greatly affect how a project proceeds. Know the assigned project’s method and contribute accordingly. Real failure is an idea that is never materialized and never receives investment.

## Chapter 5: Proposal (*起案*) Phase
The proposal phase is extremely important for business companies. Typical flow: prior-year strategy → measures → super-rough estimates → prioritize by cost-effectiveness → decide next-year projects and budgets.

The proposing *department* (not the in-house SE) owns the proposal document (*企画書*). Sequence: (1) consider measures from As-Is issues and strategy, (2) business consideration (To-Be flows, system requirements, non-system costs, expected benefits), (3) systemization aligned with IT strategy/roadmap, (4) project plan (organization and milestones only after analyses mature).

**As-Is/To-Be business flows** visualize current and future work; used for improvement points, future design, and later training. Business users own creation; in-house SE supports. Proposal phase uses high-level (abstract) flows; later phases detail them. Decide who/when/granularity in advance. Reuse existing notation.

**IT-solution consideration**: How IT can resolve issues, factoring system requirements, project purpose, IT strategy/roadmap, and existing landscape. Users requesting “XXX system” is the wrong demand; the in-house SE proposes after confirming the real problem. Compare options on business/system merits, 5-year cost, and lead time. Pursue overall (not partial) optimization; avoid silos by checking reuse, group-wide thinking, and strategy alignment. Focus on mechanisms supporting repetitive work by many people.

**Role division**: In-house SE lays rails (process, schedule, prompting) for often-inexperienced business departments without rigidly applying full PM methods. Five steps: understand counterpart, confirm internal deadlines, assess current consideration, request straw-man drafts, schedule regular meetings. If even a draft is not produced, stop—execution ability is required. The proposing department remains the project owner; IT is only a subset of measures. Low IT literacy causes dumping; raise literacy via solution seminars and lectures from companies that have already implemented.

**Ultra-rough estimates** (*超概算*): Development costs (licenses, construction, interfaces, testers, infrastructure) + operation costs (cloud, maintenance). Include buffer, travel for large projects. Combine sources (web prices, third parties, internal experts, experience, vendor inquiry—timing the last carefully). SaaS may have low construction but high running costs.

**Approval process** is a company checkpoint (*関所*), not a wasteful formality. Treat meetings as a health-check; prepare openly and want problems pointed out. *Nemawashi* (prior sharing) that raises quality is recommended. Precision expected differs by phase (proposal: rough concept/estimate; launch: outline/high; construction: specific changes).

> Beware of skipping the original purpose so that systemization (the means) becomes the purpose. For an internal SE, the real failure is a case never even made into a project.
