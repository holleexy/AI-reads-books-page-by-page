## Product Manager Role and Importance

The Product Manager (PM) is regarded as one of the world's most attractive and high-demand professions. In an era where IT-enabled products determine business survival and user problems grow increasingly complex, PMs are scarce talent responsible for guiding products to market success.

> Behind every successful product there is always an excellent product manager. PMs unify engineers, designers, and marketers; provide direction; nurture products users continue to use; and improve business revenue.

The role shares similarities with a producer in music or film: drawing out talents while bearing responsibility for both business and creative success. It is a gritty, persistent job requiring vast learning, steady execution, and sometimes facing criticism as the responsible party. No one starts as a perfect PM; growth begins from skills in one domain.

PMs focus on the *user transformation* a product should create rather than merely building and advertising. A core questioning technique is: “What change do you want to produce for users with this product?”

The book (preface dated February 2021 by Takuya Oikawa) aims to make product management thinking widely recognized, establish professional PMs as an occupation, and contribute to a better society. Product creation involves hardship, but difficulties vanish upon hearing user feedback like “I always use it” or “This changed my life.”

### HSBC PayMe Case Study (2014–2016)
HSBC was losing millennial market share amid threats from Alipay and WeChat Pay. A PM proposed a social payment app focused on Hong Kong, addressing pain points in money exchanges during social activities (e.g., meals). Validation used repeated user interviews and quantitative surveys. PayMe launched ~1 year later, exceeding user targets by 300% (50% new HSBC customers) and eventually used by over one-third of Hong Kong residents. Features emphasized social experiences and viral diffusion.

## Defining Product Success

Product success consists of three balanced elements:
- **Vision**: The worldview of the future the product wants to create. Realizing the vision is the product’s reason for existence. Teams should ensure every member can state and share the same vision.
- **User Value**: The product must make users feel value so they continue using it. Value is initially proposed to a subset of users in the vision, then expanded via validated hypotheses.
- **Business Revenue**: Required to sustain the team, investments, and progress toward the vision. Involves observing the market, launching a distinct strategy, and constructing a business model.

These elements mutually influence each other; the absence of any one causes the product to crumble. PMs maintain this balance. Temporary revenue gains that reduce user value are unsustainable. Example of coexistence: relevant, useful ads instead of flashy ones that harm UX.

> Even a product delivering large user value and revenue is unsuccessful if not heading toward its vision.

## Product Stages and Product-Market Fit (PMF)

Products have stage-specific goals. Stages are 0→1, 1→10, and 10→100 (ongoing, never-ending).

- **0→1 (Zero-to-One)**: Launching from scratch. Primary objective is achieving *Product-Market Fit (PMF)*. Without PMF, the business cannot be sustained. Focus on iterating value validation with users. Innovator-type PMs excel here. Highest priority is making the product visible via rapid hypothesis cycles. Goal: ship a prototype/MVP and find PMF before funds run out. “Do not rush hiring until PMF is achieved.”
- **1→10**: Rapid user growth after PMF. Control growth speed of business/organization alongside rising user expectations. Monetize and deliver expected features for stable revenue. Growth-type (or Growth Hacker) PMs focus here. Continuous short-term initiatives plus long-term vision alignment.
- **10→100**: Established market position but large/complex. Requires robust, accountable infrastructure (possibly global). Town-builder-type PMs balance long-term vision with diverse user needs, alliances, acquisitions, and ecosystem domination (e.g., Android OS, Salesforce). Continuous new value delivery.

PMF (coined by Andy Rachleff) is “finding a strong value hypothesis”—why users will use the product. Validated when users purchase value at the assumed price. Critical for startups and new businesses in large enterprises. Peter Thiel’s *Zero to One* emphasizes aiming for 10x better than existing solutions by questioning the status quo.

The definition of success and required viewpoints change by stage. The product lifecycle begins in earnest only after PMF.

## Product Definition and PM Responsibilities

A *product* is “something that proposes value to individuals or organizations that can become customers in the market.” The book focuses on IT-utilized products (software and hardware).

- **Narrow definition**: The deliverable/output by the product team.
- **Broad definition** (used in the book): The entire business, including sales, PR, customer support, and marketing. Boundaries between product and business have become ambiguous due to IT. Customer Success (proactive, in-product) has largely absorbed traditional support.

A single product can contain multiple functions or be split into a *Product Group* sharing a vision (e.g., SNS + Messenger). This allows clear PM responsibility scopes. In multi-PM organizations, one PM may own the group while others own specific products or even a single feature (e.g., “like” button) as a mini-CEO.

PMs are involved in mid-to-long-term strategy, vision, and all processes (business, development, UX). They make product decisions after stakeholder approval but must delegate to avoid becoming a bottleneck. Sometimes called “mini-CEOs” due to broad responsibility, but they lack personnel authority and independent fundraising. The term means possessing CEO-like passion and curiosity for product success as a personal mission.

The three essential intersecting areas for PMs:
- **Business**: Judging market user acquisition and revenue generation.
- **UX**: Discovering what users truly want and providing it usably.
- **Technology**: Judging feasibility.

PMs have two types of work: growing/nurturing the product, and unifying stakeholders while leading the team.

**Product vs. Project**: A project has a defined start/end and focuses on QCD (Quality, Cost, Delivery). A product has no predefined end and continuously delivers value. Products encompass multiple projects. In Japan, both are abbreviated PM; the book uses PM for Product Manager and PJM for Project Manager.

## The Four Hierarchical Product Layers

Products are structured in four layers (highest to lowest abstraction). Upper layers are prerequisites for lower ones; a change in an upper layer requires re-examining all below. Iterate back-and-forth (not strictly top-down). After considering one layer, perform **Fit & Refine** for consistency.

- **Core**: Product worldview (mission, vision), contribution to the company, business strategy. Highest abstraction. Discussed by PM with stakeholders.
- **Why**: Who to put into what desired state (target users, pain/gain), why the company should do it (market/competitive analysis). Driven by PM with PMM, business planning, UX researchers.
- **What**: Concrete solutions (UX: mental model, customer journey; business model: cost/revenue; roadmap, metrics, milestones). UX with designers; business model with planning.
- **How**: Realization (UI, design/implementation, Go-To-Market). PM delegates to specialists (engineers, PMM, CS) but remains accountable.

Creating a product = hypothesis verification across chained layers. Releasing a feature verifies the How-linked chain. If rejected, identify the incorrect layer.

**Fit & Refine**: After one layer, check compatibility (Fit) with the layer above and polish/improve (Refine) the higher layer. Repeating this creates a strong, consistent axis. Example: Considering What features refines Why personas.

The PM is accountable for all four layers. Clear written articulation of Core/Why/What enables team alignment.

## Six Core PM Skills

PMs require six skills that improve user value and business revenue toward vision realization. Relative importance shifts by team/product stage. Not all must be mastered initially.

- **Ideation (発想力)**: Indicate product direction; create business models; combine existing elements. Keep high information sensitivity.
- **Planning (計画力)**: Mid-term roadmap and success metrics (not Gantt charts). Prioritize ideas by user-value magnitude.
- **Execution (実行力)**: Own all layers (foundational knowledge even if not coding/designing). Assemble efforts to turn vision into action.
- **Hypothesis Verification (仮説検証力)**: Everything is a hypothesis. Rapid small-risk loops; distinguish hypotheses from facts using data.
- **Risk Management (リスク管理力)**: Estimate and address risks (legal, ethical, vulnerabilities). Avoid excessive aversion that kills speed. Align with lifecycle stage.
- **Team Building (チーム構築力)**: Align stakeholders and lead the team (soft: leadership, psychological safety, negotiation; hard: software processes, DevOps).

The six skills form a framework supporting dual paths (user value + revenue). PMs generate new value by novel combinations and stakeholder communication.

## Growing the Product: Hypotheses, Lean Canvas, and Ideation

PART II covers forming/verifying hypotheses for the four layers. Use one-page frameworks like **Lean Canvas** (Ash Maurya) for overview: left side product info, right side market info. Nine items include Problem/Existing Alternatives, Solution, Key Metrics, Unique Value Proposition (UVP)/High-Level Concept, Unfair Advantage, Channels, Customer Segments/Early Adopters, Cost Structure, Revenue Streams. Map to Core (center), Why (ends), What (sides). Verify consistency like Fit & Refine. Create accompanying schedule/milestones (but prioritize new interview insights over rigid plans).

**MVP (Minimum Viable Product)**: Smallest product that proposes core value. Release incrementally to test value, not polish everything first. Maximize limited resources via efficient verification.

**Ideation**: Decompose ideas into independent problem + solution. Objectively evaluate: Does the problem exist? Is it important? Is this the best solution? Group ideation traps: Idea Sword Fight, All-out Criticism, Self-projection Thinking, Rushing to Conclusions.

Techniques:
- Ask “why?” hard (challenge assumptions/status quo).
- Attribute decomposition.
- Mandalaart (3x3 grids generating 72 keywords).
- SCAMPER (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse).
- Pair design (15-min diverge/converge sessions).
- Change name/label; impose/remove constraints.

Generate many ideas, then select optimally (“1000 knocks” mindset).

## Product Core: Vision, Mission, and Strategy

Core is the most abstract layer: worldview (mission/vision as identity/foundation) and company contribution (business strategy).

Worldview indicates direction to users, suppliers, investors, employees. Examples:
- LinkedIn: Mission—support professionals’ work/careers worldwide. Vision—connect professionals for economic opportunities.
- Shopify: Make commerce better for everyone.

Set appropriate abstraction: unique yet broad enough for pivots. Include emotional/societal impact. Evaluate from talent-acquisition view. In startups, co-create as a team; in large orgs, small core team drafts.

**Important Things Ranking (大切なものランキング)**: Rank 1–10 non-negotiable items the team cherishes (metrics, process constraints like overtime limits, quality). Similar to trade-off sliders but linear priority. Includes expiration date and decided matters. Visualizes discussions, aligns values, supports decisions. Rebuild if a decision conflicts.

Business strategy: Define competing domain and winning path. Align with company strategy (portfolio, synergies, branding). Use **Product Portfolio Management (PPM)/BCG Matrix**: Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks, Dogs (treat as foundational, not always directly applicable to IT).

In Core, explicitly note already-decided constraints (e.g., existing cafe business launching coffee-bean subscription).

Case study (publisher launching BtoC 0→1 product): Company mission—“Expand people’s possibilities.” Vision—“Open up a future where everyone can thrive.” Derived product mission—“Create a new channel that becomes the encounter between people and books.” Provisional vision—“Necessary knowledge before it becomes necessary.”

## Product Why: Target Users, Value, and Analysis

Why examines purpose: who to put in what state (from vision), and why the company (strengths, market/competitive analysis).

**Users vs. Customers**: Users actually use the product (multiple types possible, e.g., recipe writers + readers). Customers hold payment/decision authority (e.g., parents for children’s app; systems dept for B2B).

**Value Proposition Canvas (VPC)**: Left—Value Map (Products/Services, Gain Creators, Pain Relievers). Right—Customer Profile (Jobs, Gains, Pains). One canvas per persona. Start with Customer Profile (list day’s activities or work tasks; interview real people). Pains/gains often two sides of a coin—probe root causes. Check left-right consistency.

Methodologies: MVP, VPC, Pain/Gain analysis, user interviews; PEST, Five Forces, SWOT, Cross-SWOT, STP.

**External Analysis**:
- **PEST**: Politics, Economy, Society, Technology (e.g., copyright amendments, declining print sales, e-books, AI).
- **Five Forces**: Buyers, Sellers, New Entrants, Substitutes, Competitors.
- **SWOT**: Internal Strengths/Weaknesses + External Opportunities/Threats (populate Opportunities/Threats from PEST). Kodak example: possessed digital camera tech but neglected it due to film-business conflict, leading to bankruptcy.
- **Cross-SWOT**: Combine into strategies (S×O leverage strengths for opportunities; O×W use opportunities to overcome weaknesses; etc.).

**Market-in vs. Product-out**: Use both to find fit. Competitors = same market; Substitutes = same value, different market.

**STP**: Segmentation (decompose market), Targeting (6R: Rank, Realistic, Reach, Response, Rate, Rival), Positioning (2×2 matrix on winning axes).

**User Interviews**: At least two types—(1) early pain/gain validation, (2) later solution validation. Thorough prep (script, NDA, icebreaker). Use open 5W1H questions; avoid leading/yes-no. Address social-desirability bias. Capture non-verbal cues (prefer live voice). Paid interviews more serious but risk pleasing answers. Summarize promptly (KJ method/affinity diagrams). Celebrate early invalidation. Follow with quantitative surveys for market size.

After Why, perform Fit & Refine vs. Core. Do not forget vision—building only what current users want prevents innovation (chimera product example: adding fins, legs, facial features without unifying vision).

Case study continuation: Brainstormed user-value pairs classified in 4-quadrant (specialized vs. broad knowledge; knowledge vs. emotion). Vision-fit favored knowledge-seeking. Personas created (e.g., high-expertise 35yo PM, overworked 32yo, ambitious 27yo). VPC/Value Maps converged on quality-information filtering/push app. PEST/SWOT/Cross-SWOT informed strategies (e.g., curate individual content, summaries). Lean Canvas for competitors (book EC site, news app). STP: axes “recommendation criteria” and “information freshness.” Interviews refined to working adults with defined domains; excluded students. Vision refined to “knowledge needed for growth before it becomes necessary.”

## Product What: UX, Business Model, Roadmap, and Metrics

What = solutions realizing Why (UX + business model + sequencing via roadmap/metrics). Countless possible Whats; select most effective (“1000 knocks”). Design UX and business model lockstep with same premises.

**UX** (full journey from awareness to purpose achievement) vs. **UI** (visible/tangible interaction). UX as continuous sequence, not isolated points. Broad scope (triggers, emotional changes). PM treats UX as own responsibility.

- **Persona**: Fictional user from target segment, grounded in real data. Includes name, photo, attributes, daily life. One per distinct role. Not the entire segment.
- **User Goals**: End (accomplish task), Experience (emotions/motivations), Life (ultimate influence).
- **Mental Model**: Implicit assumptions (“it will work like this”). Diagram: user behaviors (upper) mapped to functions (lower) in “towers.” Reveals gaps.
- **Customer Journey Map (CJM)**: Timeline of actions/thoughts (upper) vs. product functions (lower). Visualizes transitions, decision reasons, churn. Per persona. Like time-series VPC.
- **Wireframes**: Simple UI/UX expression (hand-drawn or tool) to convey intent to designers (not constrain them).

**Business Model**: “Logical description of what value to create and how to deliver it” (Osterwalder/Pigneur). **Business Model Canvas (BMC)**: 9 blocks, fill right-to-left (user-centric): Customer Segments → Value Proposition → Channels → Customer Relationships → Revenue Streams → Key Resources → Key Activities → Key Partners → Cost Structure.

**Roadmap**: Itinerary with intermediate *milestones* (waypoints) toward vision. Communication tool showing position, outlook, interlocking initiatives. Continuously update. Phasing (gradual implementation, e.g., MVP first). Visualize publicly. Accompanied by shared success metrics.

**Metrics**:
- **KPIs**: Numerical, comparable, time-series, internal, SMART (Specific, Measurable, Agreeable/Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). Lagging (outcomes) vs. Leading (predictors).
- **KGI**: Final business goal (e.g., revenue).
- **KPI Tree**: Decompose KGI into KPIs.
- **North Star Metric (NSM)**: Single metric measuring if core value is delivered to users. Leading indicator preceding revenue; linked to UX/retention; understandable; hard to game. Zoom example: weekly meetings hosted. Decompose into Topline KPIs then Sub KPIs (MECE, shallow, cross-checked vs. CJM). Position NSM as product-specific KGI.

After What, Fit & Refine vs. Why. Check technical/resource/IP/privacy feasibility. Confirm market size via STP. Verify model acceptance (e.g., dynamic pricing may fail in B2B due to approval processes).

Case study: Combined personas into “A-ko” (31yo female PM). Mental model → CJM for search. Wireframe for hashtag search. BMC: two-sided platform (writers/readers); revenue via freemium + ads/book commissions. Roadmap: Jan β (MVP, ads only) → July official → Nov billing. NSM: total user viewing time (satisfied 5 criteria). Decomposed into users, frequency, time-per-user; then KPIs (views, engagement, content volume, retention). Lean Canvas updated. Core value: systematize dynamic information.
