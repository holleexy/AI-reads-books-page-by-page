## Origins of the Methodology

The author's sales methodology and large-scale research (surveys totaling ~24,000 people plus support for over 40,000 salespeople) originated from the **frustration of losing deals** (*失注の悔しさ*). At age 25, entering *法人営業* (corporate/B2B sales) without mentors, they self-taught through daily trial and error. A competitive personality made losses physically debilitating.

A pivotal loss over 20 years ago—selling new-employee training to a large IT vendor—revealed the customer's hidden world. After rejection, an intense post-loss meeting uncovered a 24-criteria Excel scoring sheet (max 120 points) used internally. The author ranked outside the top 3. The winner had no official track record but had long served as an informal *discussion partner*, building relationships with HR, field department heads, and budget holders **before** the customer even recognized the need or issued an RFP.

> Late-stage engagement limited to the official gatekeeper places a vendor at a severe disadvantage. Early, multi-stakeholder relationship-building before an RFP exists can create an insurmountable lead.

**Core principle:** If you cannot see what is happening behind the customer, even the most excellent product or proposal will never bear fruit. Winning patterns built on thorough understanding of the customer's hidden *worldview* (*世界観*) and *backside* (*裏側*) are rock-solid.

## Foundational Frameworks: Illuminating the Customer's Hidden World

The standard 4-stage sales process (approach → hearing → presentation → closing) is **completely different** from the customer's 5-stage purchasing process. Salespeople plan to compete at estimate/closing while the customer has often already decided.

### The 5-Stage Purchasing Process
1. *課題認識* (Issue Recognition) — Business issue arises; “some purchase might be necessary.”
2. *情報収集* (Information Gathering) — Light research on options; temperature still low.
3. *要件定義* (Requirements Definition) — Budget, functions, timeline, operations verbalized.
4. *ベンダー比較* (Vendor Comparison) — Formal proposals and estimates compared.
5. *発注決定* (Order Decision) — Vendor selected and contract signed (often ceremonial).

The actual decision frequently occurs in upstream stages. Once the customer’s mind is made up, later sales effort is often wasted.

### The 6 Types of Stakeholders
Each has distinct roles, priorities, and judgment criteria. One person often occupies multiple roles. Influence is dispersed, fluid, and frequently invisible.

- **Field users** (*現場の利用者*): “Fast and cheap,” usable in daily work, low departmental burden. Strong “usable?” orientation.
- **Internal promoters** (*社内推進者*): Accurate *issue grasping*; want project success. High weight on “does this vendor understand our problems?”
- **Ordering personnel** (*発注担当者*): Vendor coordination, quotes, contracts. Balancing mindset; dispersed criteria.
- **Budget supervisors** (*予算の監督者*): Cost-effectiveness, track record, peace of mind. Conservative “brake” function.
- **Influencers to the decision-maker** (*決裁者への影響者*): Fit with company situation. Low visibility but high impact (“What does that person say?”).
- **Final decision-maker** (*最終決裁者*): Investment worth / ROI, not cheapest price. Must later justify “this was the right investment.”

Formal authority (*kessai-ken*) ≠ actual influence.

### The 4 Mazes (*4つの迷路*)
Salespeople fall into these because they cannot see the customer’s internal world:

1. Unable to meet the key person
2. Unable to make contact / unresponsive
3. Consideration/review does not progress
4. Budget does not increase

A *maze* is a situation where the salesperson does not know which stakeholders exist or the current internal situation, so time simply passes.

**The Torch (*松明*)** to escape: (1) Accurately identify the current purchasing stage, (2) Determine the role of the person in front of you, (3) Map a clear path so the organization decides to order from you.

## Defensive Strategies: Escaping the Mazes

Correctly diagnose *which maze and which specific pitfall* rather than repeating the same tactics (more calls, more follow-ups).

### Maze 1: Unable to Meet the Key Person
9 pitfalls. The “key person” is not always the formal DM and can shift by stage.

Tactics: *Bead-stringing* (*数珠つなぎ*) — chain through multiple stakeholders rather than aiming straight at the DM. Live influence-chain mapping in the meeting. Same-day forwardable memos. Never bypass the contact (iron rule: stand with the person in front of you first). Reduce psychological burden (many candidate dates, “we handle all prep”). Tailor materials to the KP’s current interests.

### Maze 2: Contact Does Not Connect
9 pitfalls (unread, thinking-stop from info overload, poor personal impression, value not conveyed, no time, memory faded, internal confirmation needed, poor verbalization, fear of triggering sales pressure).

Tactics: Value-adding emails (never “how about it?” reminders). Multiple-choice replies (answer with a number). Involve a superior. Send editable drafts (not PDF). Propose 10-minute calls. Explicitly state “no rush / no decision needed now.” Tailor content and tone by stakeholder role. Form hypotheses from prior meeting clues and test.

> No reply ≠ no interest. Customer silence often has reasons unrelated to the salesperson or proposal quality.

### Maze 3: Consideration Does Not Progress
10 pitfalls — individual (ball not passed, no personal buy-in, skill gap, specific opponent, postponing DM) vs. organizational (higher-priority issues, unidentified stakeholders, too busy to convene, vague judgment criteria, no mechanism to reconcile opinions).

**Accompany (*伴走する*)**, do not wait or urge. Co-create internal tools, co-design the sequence, think together when obstacles appear.

*Three-eyes framework*: *鳥の目* (bird’s-eye / management alignment), *虫の目* (insect’s-eye / field specifics), *魚の目* (fish’s-eye / industry trends).

Buyer types (survey n=10,303): Logical 27.2%, Emotional 14.9%, Political 57.9%. Over half are political (“is it safe? what will others think?”).

### Maze 4: Budget Does Not Increase
9 pitfalls — personal (business-side, management-side, inter-departmental), procedural (stuck before entering process, cumbersome steps, busy handler), cultural (in-house preference, start-small, strict ROI).

Use *予算すごろく* (Budget Sugoroku) map of the 4-layer communication hierarchy (management → business → department → individual). Co-create approval materials (IKEA effect — people value what they help create). Do **not** discount or shrink the proposal; help construct the internal logic and evidence to *create* additional budget. Quantify ROI using anonymized past-client before/after data and customized simulations.

> “No budget” is usually a one-word summary of hidden internal circumstances, not an actual lack of funds.

## Offensive Strategies: Winning Patterns Mapped to Purchasing Stages

Shift from reactive “escape the maze” to proactive: identify the customer’s current stage and apply the matching pattern. 66.8% of deals are decided upstream — first-mover advantage is decisive.

### Engagement Strategy (Issue Recognition Stage)
Nurture relationships *before* BANT conditions exist. Create the feeling “this proposal is perfectly timed for us.” Relationship-building engine: cycle of being helpful → gaining understanding.

Manage at *lead person phases* (individual, not company level): Initial → Contact → Needs Grasp → Interested → Opportunity. Prioritize via company profile + individual profile + ICP (Ideal Customer Profile extracted from actual successful closed-won deals). Extended BANTCH (adds Competitor + Human resources).

### Expert Strategy (Information Gathering Stage)
Become the “safe choice to consult.” Build a knowledge system: typical customer worries → conventional approaches → their pitfalls → unique proposed approach + why only we can execute it + concrete steps.

5 C’s content framework (Company, Customer, Customer’s Customer, Competitor, Customer’s Competitor). Verbalize via speaking/口述筆記 then light polish (preserves originality vs. generic AI). Target high *problem-solving degree* × high *rarity*. Run continuous enlightenment cycle (verbalize → emit → analyze reactions). 1st/2nd/3rd-team content roster for testing.

### Discussion Strategy (Requirements Definition Stage)
Become the accompanying discussion partner so the customer feels “I don’t want to buy from anyone else — we’ve already built so much together.” Low-risk, high-return. Invest resources *before* the deal materializes.

Create two-way field (*つっこまれビリティ* / poke-ability, IKEA participation). Two-mountains meeting structure. 5W2H deep-dive + unravel assumptions to generate *discoveries*. Organize requirements (keywords, completeness, concretization, priority). Coverless working drafts. Follow-up minutes + personal memo.

### Guide Strategy (Vendor Comparison Stage)
Differentiate via **process**, not just proposal content. Become the *pathfinder* (*道先案内人*) who makes internal consideration smoother.

Map customer’s position on Budget Sugoroku. Grasp organizational issues across 4 hierarchical layers. Influence map (title vs. stance vs. actual influence + 6 roles). Decision structure as “options × judgment axes.” Use MEDDICC (Metrics, Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, Decision Process, Identify Pain, Champion, Competition) as an information-war tool. Maintain Account Plans for large enterprises. Shape the customer’s judgment criteria themselves.

### Customize Strategy (Order Decision Stage)
Create *特別感* (sense of specialness) — “they really thought this through specifically for us.” Last-mile differentiator when functional/price differences are small.

Three layers:
- **Request response**: Exhaustive requirement organization (comprehensiveness + concretization + priorities) + unrequested extras + visual correspondence mapping. Especially powerful in replacement deals (inherit good + overlay improvements).
- **Cause analysis**: Show the analysis process (not just conclusions), visualize vicious/virtuous cycles, apply customized frameworks. Pair facts with messages. Frame challenges as “flip side of existing strengths / next-stage preparation.”
- **Proposal message**: Kill two birds (spillover benefits), long-term scope (achievable future + milestones), specific Before → After impact (reverse demo to raise resolution of current pain).

Survey (n=2,676): Top “final push” is ROI / concrete benefits (22.1%).

## Overarching Principles and Scaling

- Response speed frequently outweighs proposal quality because it enables the internal champion to meet sudden executive deadlines.
- Most customers already know they should act; external pressure/urgency-inciting tactics rank among the *least* effective.
- Fast first (even imperfect) estimate + rapid iteration beats one-shot “perfect” proposal.
- Cost-effectiveness remains the #1 criterion across *all* stages; relative price vs. competitors rises later; support/delivery certainty rises late.
- Formal quotes are often an internal governance ritual even after the favorite is already chosen.
- To scale from individual to team: replace intuition with shared, backwards-from-purchasing-process systems and a common language.

> There are mysterious wins, but no mysterious losses. Lost deals always have identifiable root causes that can be uncovered by seeing the customer’s hidden world.

The book is structured in three parts: Part 1 (preparatory frameworks so you don’t panic in the maze), Part 2 (defensive escape tactics), Part 3 (offensive stage-specific winning patterns). The ultimate goal is making invisible stakeholders and unique purchasing processes visible so any salesperson can independently decide “what to do next.”
